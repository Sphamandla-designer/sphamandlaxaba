Constraints

Design decisions happen inside real-world limits.

  • Existing brand

    The CMAXX mark, orange and dark navy were fixed. The palette had to carry a technical, security-adjacent tone without becoming aggressive.

  • Client-supplied content

    Service names, technology partners and trust markers came from the business. My job was to structure and edit them, not to invent them.

  • Platform

    WordPress + Elementor. Layouts had to be reproducible by the client's own team after handover, which ruled out anything I couldn't rebuild in the page builder.

  • Breadth of offering

    Seven service lines across four divisions, plus sector and location pages — all of which had to fit in one navigation without a mega-menu the client couldn't maintain.

  • Single-designer engagement

    No research budget and no separate development team. Every decision below is a designer's judgement call taken with the client, then shipped.

02 — The problem

CMAXX does four different jobs for five different kinds of customer. The website had to make that legible.

WiFi design, CCTV and security, structured cabling and infrastructure, cloud and Microsoft 365 — sold to homeowners, offices, restaurants, retail and events. Everything below is drawn from the service offering and the site itself, not from user research.

Business need

Be credible enough to be trusted with infrastructure.

CMAXX competes with individual technicians on one side and enterprise integrators on the other. The site needed to carry a multi-division offering, real trust markers (BBBEE Level 1, 9+ years, 100+ projects, supported technologies) and a single obvious way to start a conversation.

User need

Recognise my problem before I learn your service names.

A visitor rarely arrives searching for "structured cabling". They arrive with a dead zone in the back bedroom, a slow connection they are already paying for, or a security gap they have been warned about. The vocabulary gap between customer symptom and service category is the core UX problem here.

Design opportunity

How might we make a broad, technical offering easier to understand, navigate and act on?

Framed as a structure problem first and a visual problem second: if the categories and the paths between them are right, the interface has much less to compensate for.

The four symptoms the content leads with — in the customer's words, not ours

Dead zone

Rooms the WiFi doesn't reach — the most common reason someone starts looking.

Slow speed

Paying for fast internet and not getting it. The network, not the ISP.

Security vulnerability

An unsecured or misconfigured network, with CCTV and cloud systems attached.

Business downtime

Unreliable connectivity costing a team real hours.

03 — My UX approach

Structuring the experience

The interface came out of a structure, in this order. Each step constrained the next — which is why the final pages look inevitable rather than composed.

  1. Step 01

    Service ecosystem

    List the whole offering honestly before grouping anything: four divisions, seven service lines.

  2. Step 02

    Information architecture

    Group by what a customer would recognise, not by how the business is organised internally.

  3. Step 03

    User intent

    Three broad intents — home, business, technical evaluation — each needing a different depth of information.

  4. Step 04

    Content hierarchy

    Symptom first, service second, specification third. Trust markers placed where doubt appears.

  5. Step 05

    Conversion journey

    One primary action — the free assessment — reachable from every depth of the site.

The website needed to accommodate a broad service ecosystem without forcing visitors to understand the company's internal structure. I grouped the offering into service lines a customer can self-select from, and kept sector-based and location-based routes as separate entry points rather than folding them into the service menu.

03C — The trust layer

18 supported technologies, one strip.

Eighteen vendor brands across WiFi, CCTV, cabling and smart home — a real asset the business wanted visible, and a real layout problem: logos of wildly different weight and aspect ratio that still had to read as one credential rather than a wall of badges. I normalised them into a single low-contrast strip so the set reads as scope, and no one vendor outranks another.

03D — Representative journeys

Three intents the structure has to serve.

Representative journeys derived from the service structure and intended experience — not validated user research.

Intent 01

Find a home WiFi solution

  1. →Recognise the symptom — dead zone or slow speed
  2. →Read the matching service line
  3. →Build confidence — reviews, BBBEE, technologies
  4. →Request a free assessment

The shortest path in the site. Most likely to arrive on a phone.

Intent 02

Find a business connectivity solution

  1. →Arrive with an operational problem, often downtime
  2. →Explore the business-facing services
  3. →Cross-check the sector page for their environment
  4. →Enquire, or call directly

Higher intent, longer consideration. Needs both the sector route and the service route to exist.

Intent 03

Evaluate a technical solution

  1. →Arrive already knowing the vocabulary
  2. →Go straight to infrastructure or cloud detail
  3. →Check supported technologies and support model
  4. →Contact a specialist

This visitor wants specification, not reassurance — which is why detail is kept, just deferred.

04 — Key design decisions

Four decisions, and what would prove them right or wrong.

Each block reads the same way: the problem, the decision, the reasoning, what shipped, what actually supports it — and the test I would run next. Where there is no evidence, the block says so.

DECISION 01

Lead with the customer's problem, not the service catalogue

Homepage, above the services grid

Problem

A visitor arriving from search has a symptom, not a service name. Opening with a seven-item catalogue asks them to translate their own problem into our vocabulary before anything is useful.

Decision

Place a 'Common problems we solve' section — dead zone, slow speed, security vulnerability, business downtime — ahead of the services grid, and let each symptom act as an entry point toward the relevant service line.

Why

Recognition is cheaper than recall. If the first thing on the page names the visitor's situation in their own words, the rest of the page is read as relevant rather than browsed as a menu. It also gives the sales conversation a shared starting point.

Interface — what shipped

  • ▸Four symptom cards in the customer's language
  • ▸'Is your WiFi or network letting you down?' as the section framing
  • ▸A single 'Speak to a specialist' action for people who don't want to self-diagnose
  • ▸Services grid placed immediately after, so the translation happens on one screen

Evidence / limitation

Supported by the service offering and how enquiries are described by the business — not by user research. No testing was run on the ordering.

Next validation

A/B the symptom-first order against a services-first order, measuring scroll depth to the services grid and assessment-form starts.

DECISION 02

Progressive disclosure: overview page, then service detail

Services grid → 7 service pages

Problem

Seven service lines, each with four to five sub-services and its own technical detail. Putting that on one page buries the visitor; splitting it too finely makes the site feel bottomless.

Decision

Two levels only. The homepage grid gives each service line a one-sentence description plus its sub-services as a scannable list; the dedicated page carries the depth, inclusions and technology detail.

Why

The overview answers 'is this relevant to me?' at a glance; the detail page answers 'is this the right level of solution?' for the visitor who has already self-selected. Technical content stays available without taxing the visitor who doesn't need it.

Interface — what shipped

  • ▸Service cards with a description plus 4–5 named sub-services
  • ▸One 'Explore service' action per card — no competing links
  • ▸Seven dedicated pages, one level deep, mirrored in the Services submenu
  • ▸Sector and location pages kept as separate routes rather than nested inside services

Evidence / limitation

The structure is visible in the shipped IA and navigation. No card-sort or tree-test was run to validate the grouping.

Next validation

A tree test on the seven category names — can someone place 'my office CCTV isn't recording' in the right service line without help?

DECISION 03

Contextual CTAs repeated through the journey

Header, hero, every section, every service page, footer

Problem

Enquiry is the only conversion on this site, and visitors reach readiness at different depths — some in the hero, some after the technologies list, some only after reading a service page.

Decision

Keep one primary action — 'Get a free assessment' — and repeat it in the local language of each section, with a phone/WhatsApp alternative always beside it rather than hidden on the contact page.

Why

A single repeated action avoids splitting intent across competing CTAs, and the assessment framing lowers commitment: it asks for a diagnosis, not a purchase. Offering a call alongside it respects that a lot of South African service enquiries happen by phone or WhatsApp.

Interface — what shipped

  • ▸'Get a free assessment' as the standing primary action
  • ▸'Call us now' / WhatsApp as a persistent secondary
  • ▸Section-level variants — 'Speak to a specialist', 'Explore service' — that all resolve to the same funnel
  • ▸Header 'Get in touch' pinned across every page and breakpoint

Evidence / limitation

CTA placement is verifiable on the live site. Nothing about relative CTA performance is known — no analytics have been shared.

Next validation

Instrument each CTA position and compare assessment-form starts by section, then cut the placements that earn nothing.

DECISION 04

Design the mobile navigation of a deep service menu first

All breakpoints, mobile-led

Problem

Nine top-level destinations, one of which expands into seven service pages. On a phone that is a menu that either hides everything important or scrolls past the fold.

Decision

Design the mobile menu as the constraint and let desktop inherit it: a compact drawer with Services expanding in place, contact details surfaced inside the menu, and the primary CTA fixed above it.

Why

The majority of visitors arrive on a phone, often mid-problem. If the deepest part of the IA works in a drawer, the desktop version is easy — the reverse is not true. It also forced the service names to stay short enough to read at 320px.

Interface — what shipped

  • ▸Single drawer with in-place Services expansion — no nested pages
  • ▸Phone, WhatsApp and email inside the menu, not only in the footer
  • ▸Stacked service cards preserving the sub-service lists at 320px
  • ▸Tap targets and CTA kept above 44px throughout

Evidence / limitation

Behaviour is verifiable in the live implementation at each breakpoint. No device analytics or mobile-specific testing has been shared with me.

Next validation

Check real device split and mobile menu engagement, then test whether surfacing the two or three most-requested services directly in the drawer beats a single Services expander.

05 — Interface

Opportunity → design decision → final interface

CMAXX had no previous website, so there is no honest "before" to compare against. Instead, each screen is shown against the opportunity it was answering.

Screen 01 — Homepage

Five questions, answered in order.

Opportunity

A first-time visitor needs to work out what CMAXX does, whether it applies to them, what is on offer, whether the company can be trusted, and what to do next — without scrolling back up.

Design decision

Sequence the page against those five questions and let each section answer exactly one: positioning, symptoms, services, trust, contact.

  1. 1What does CMAXX do — hero positioning plus three trust markers
  2. 2Is this relevant to me — the four symptoms
  3. 3What's available — the service grid
  4. 4Why trust them — BBBEE, 9+ years, 100+ projects, supported technologies
  5. 5What next — assessment, call, WhatsApp

Screen 02 — Service discovery

Understand the offering before being asked to enquire.

Opportunity

The services grid is where the breadth either reads as capability or as confusion.

Design decision

One card per service line, each carrying a plain-language description and its named sub-services, so the visitor can scan for their own words before committing to a click.

  1. ·Equal visual weight across service lines — no hidden hierarchy
  2. ·Sub-services named, not summarised
  3. ·A single 'Explore service' action per card
  4. ·Cross-cutting offers (Smart Home, Public WiFi) given their own bands
Add: services section screenshot

Screen 03 — Service detail

Depth for the visitor who has already self-selected.

Opportunity

Someone who clicked into a service has narrowed their intent — they want inclusions, scope and evidence, not another pitch.

Design decision

Open with the value in one line, then inclusions, then supporting technical information, then the assessment CTA. Detail is deferred, never deleted.

  1. ·Headline stating the outcome, not the technology
  2. ·Inclusions as a scannable list
  3. ·Technical specifics kept below the decision-making content
  4. ·Assessment CTA repeated at the end of the page
Add: a service detail page screenshot

Screen 04 — Contact & conversion

Reduce the cost of starting a conversation.

Opportunity

The enquiry is the only conversion. Anything that adds doubt or effort at this point undoes the rest of the page.

Design decision

Offer the form plus every direct channel the business actually answers — two numbers, WhatsApp, email, location — and keep the form short enough to complete on a phone.

  1. ·Short form, phone-friendly field order
  2. ·Landline, mobile, WhatsApp and email all visible
  3. ·Location stated for local-trust reasons
  4. ·No conversion claims — nothing has been measured

06 — Responsive & production

One experience, different contexts.

Most CMAXX traffic is a person on a phone who just discovered a problem. Mobile was designed as its own set of decisions, not a narrower desktop.

320 – 767px

Mobile
  • ·Drawer navigation with Services expanding in place
  • ·Single-column stack; service cards keep their sub-service lists
  • ·Hero shortened to positioning, trust markers and both CTAs
  • ·Contact details surfaced inside the menu

768 – 1023px

Tablet
  • ·Two-column service and sector grids
  • ·Hero panel moves below the copy rather than beside it
  • ·Trust markers wrap into two rows
  • ·Section padding reduced to keep sections one screen tall

1024px +

Desktop
  • ·Full horizontal navigation with the Services submenu
  • ·Three-column service grid; hero copy beside the network panel
  • ·Contact bar with phone and email in the top strip
  • ·Larger editorial type scale for section headings

From design to production.

I designed and built this site myself in WordPress with Elementor — which means every design decision had to survive a real CMS, a real content set and a real client review, not just a canvas. Design is not finished when the design file is.

  1. Design

    IA, content structure, page layouts and the component patterns the client's team would reuse.

  2. Implementation

    Built in WordPress with Elementor by me — layouts, responsive rules, navigation and forms.

  3. QA

    Checked at 320, 768, 1024 and 1440; navigation, forms, links and content passes before launch.

  4. Iteration

    Content and section-level changes after launch as the offering and trust markers were updated.

  5. Live

    In production and still being iterated — including new service and location pages.

What I learned

Complexity needs structure before styling

Most of the value on this project was decided before any layout existed — in grouping seven service lines so a stranger could self-select.

The customer's vocabulary beats the company's

Leading with 'dead zone' rather than 'wireless coverage optimisation' was the single change that made the offering legible.

Shipping it myself changed the design

Building in Elementor meant every pattern had to be reproducible by the client afterwards. That constraint improved the system — fewer, more reusable blocks.

What I would improve next

  • →Validate the representative journeys with real users
  • →Instrument and measure enquiry conversion
  • →Tree-test the seven service category names
  • →Test CTA hierarchy and cut placements that earn nothing
  • →Analyse service-discovery entry points
  • →Sharpen page content against real search intent
  • →Iterate the mobile drawer using behavioural data
