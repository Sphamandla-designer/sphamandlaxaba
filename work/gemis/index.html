Part 01

The story behind the experience

The opportunity, the strategy, the defining decision and the identity it rests on.

01The opportunity

A strong reputation, without a front door.

GEMIS had more than a decade of work behind it, and no front door a stranger could find.

GEMIS is a South African software and business-systems company, operating since 2014. Its reputation was built through referrals and long relationships, and many clients have stayed for years.

That model works inside a network and stalls outside it. A prospective client who heard the name had nowhere to discover GEMIS independently, understand what it builds, judge whether it was credible and decide to get in touch. There was also no complete brand to express: navy was a preference, not a system.

So the opportunity was larger than a website. It was a single digital touchpoint, with an identity behind it, that could carry a visitor from discovery to enquiry without a referral doing the work.

02Discovery & strategy

Starting with the business, not the homepage.

Before drawing a screen, I worked out what the site had to achieve, for whom, and within which limits.

I began with the business manager and the full-stack developer: objectives, target clients, positioning, existing brand assets, budget, scope and technical constraints.

I reviewed comparable software and technology companies for four things: how they group services, what they show to be believed, how they present past work, and when they invite contact. It set the conventions visitors already expect, and showed where GEMIS could be plainer.

From those conversations I drafted audience profiles, labelled as stakeholder-informed working assumptions rather than research findings. Each pointed at the same needs: understand what GEMIS does, find a relevant service, judge its experience, and start a conversation easily.

The strategy came down to one question and six tensions the design had to resolve.

03Experience design

Clarity over a 3D showcase.

The defining decision was to recommend against the direction the business first asked for.

The business’s first preference was futuristic: extensive 3D, animation and scroll effects. The first build explored exactly that, with a preloader, a real-time 3D object and scroll-driven sequences.

I evaluated it against who the site was for. People assessing a technology partner want to understand the company quickly; heavy assets add performance risk where connections vary; and constant motion competes with the information people came for.

I recommended a restrained, clarity-first direction. The business gave up some spectacle and gained a site where nothing stands between a visitor and the content. Character moved into the gem, the hatch-line imagery and the navy bands.

The structure followed the same logic: seven destinations ordered by the questions a client asks, a home page that walks the journey from Discover to Enquire, and one action, Book a discovery session, on every page.

04Brand & design system

From a colour preference to a shared foundation.

GEMIS needed an identity it could use everywhere, not a website palette.

I started from the one thing that existed, a preference for navy, and built the identity around it: a cut gem in flat facets beside a tracked wordmark, legible at favicon size and in one colour, with Montserrat for its range from a light display cut to a firm label.

The identity then became a system: colour and type tokens, a square uppercase button in three treatments, a numbered section pattern, navigation with drop-downs and a full-height phone menu, and form fields with a visible error state. The same parts produced the email signatures and the social share card.

Because the parts are documented as tokens and rules rather than as finished pages, the same foundation can carry the next interface. GEMIS formally adopted it as the company’s design system.

Part 02

The experience in practice

The live website, how it reached development, and what it delivered.

05The website experience

Every page answers one question.

The live site, as a prospective client meets it on a desk or a phone.

Each page is composed around the question a client brings to it: what do you do, can you build what I need, have you done it before, will you still be here, and how do I start. Pages open with the answer and the proof beside it, then the detail, then the next step.

Interface decisions stay consistent. The current page is always marked, including on the Solutions and Products drop-downs. Anything that advances on its own can be paused. On a phone, navigation folds into a full-height menu and fields stack without losing the 44-pixel targets.

The enquiry is the end of every journey, so it got the most care: one required decision, checks as each field is left, and after a failed send an error summary that lists every problem and links to its field.

Proposition, proof and two actions in the first view. Nothing moves until the visitor does.

Nine disciplines in plain names, all connected to one accountable team.

Company figures first, then the systems in production, each with its story.

06From design to development

I led design. The developers built production.

The handoff was designed as carefully as the screens.

I designed the screens, the design-system assets and components in Figma, organised for handoff. The development team was less familiar with navigating Figma, so I also built a working HTML/CSS reference of the design and shared it as a GitHub repository: every page, its responsive behaviour, the menu, the form and its error states, with the system as tokens in one stylesheet.

The reference did not replace the team’s codebase. The full-stack developer and the development team built and host the production site; the live pages share the reference’s structure and file names.

Before handoff I ran a UX review on the reference and fixed what it found. After handoff I reviewed the implementation against the approved design and took part in testing: consistency, responsive layouts, navigation and links, calls to action, interactive elements and content.

UX review on the reference: what changed

  • Four names for one action→One name everywhere: Book a discovery session
  • Validation only on submit, no summary→Checks on leaving a field, and an error summary that takes focus
  • Current page not marked→Marked in the navigation, including the drop-down triggers
  • Auto-advancing stages could not be paused→A pause control on each
  • Body copy at 14px→16px body, with a readable line length

07Outcomes & reflection

What was delivered, and what I would change.

Confirmed outcomes only.

  1. 01A formally adopted design systemTokens, components and patterns adopted by GEMIS.
  2. 02A complete brand identityLogo, lock-ups, colour, type, email signatures and a share card.
  3. 03A live corporate websitegemis.co.za, built and hosted by the development team.
  4. 04An inspectable handoffOrganised Figma assets and a working HTML/CSS reference on GitHub.

The most useful thing I did was ask what the site was for before deciding how it should look. That question turned a website request into brand work, and gave me grounds to recommend a calmer direction than the one first requested.

Two things I would do differently. I would engage prospective users earlier: the audience profiles came from the business and the market, and a few short comprehension sessions before the structure was fixed would have tested them directly. And I would agree a post-launch measurement plan from the start, covering enquiries, service discovery and content engagement, so the site’s effect could be read rather than assumed.

A retrospective usability study on the live site is planned.

Evidence and limits

Screens are the live site, captured November 2026, or the HTML/CSS reference and its first build. Diagrams are reconstructed from the live site and the project brief. Audience profiles were working assumptions. No traffic, conversion, revenue or user-testing results are claimed.
