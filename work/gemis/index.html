02·Business contextWhat was the problem?

A strong network, and no front door.

GEMIS is a South African software and business systems company, operating since 2014. Most of its new work came through referrals and long-standing client relationships, and many of those relationships had grown into long-term partnerships.

That network was an asset, but it had a limit. Someone outside it had nowhere to go to learn what GEMIS does, see what it has built and decide whether to get in touch. There was no website experience that brought the company’s capabilities, history and work together for a first-time visitor.

The opportunity was to build that front door: a place where a prospective client could discover GEMIS on their own, understand what it offers and judge whether it could support their organisation.

04·Discovery and stakeholder alignmentWhat did you investigate?

Starting with the business, not the homepage.

I began with the business manager and the full-stack developer: what the business wanted the site to achieve, how new clients currently found GEMIS, where the company stood today, and what budget, scope and technical limits the work had to respect.

One finding shaped everything after it. GEMIS did not have a fully developed brand identity. Navy was the existing colour preference, but there was no complete visual system behind it. The website could not be designed well until there was an identity for it to express, so brand work became part of the project.

06·UX strategy and information architectureWhat did you decide?

A structure a first-time visitor can predict.

I grouped the content around the questions a prospective client asks, in the order they ask them: what do you do, how do you work, what have you built, who are you, and how do I start. Each question became a top-level destination. Services are presented as nine disciplines under one accountable team, so a visitor sees the range without having to understand the company’s internal structure.

Navigation model

  • Seven primary destinations, with Solutions and Products as drop-downs so their detail stays one step away.
  • One action at the end of the bar on every page, so the route to an enquiry never depends on where someone lands.
  • The current page is marked in the navigation, and pages three levels deep carry a breadcrumb.
  • On phones the same structure folds into a full-height menu, with the action as its last item.

07·User journeys and interaction designWhy did you make that decision?

From a first visit to a first conversation, including what goes wrong.

  1. 01DiscoverHome hero

    What GEMIS builds, for whom, on what platform, and the proof beside it.

  2. 02UnderstandSolutions

    Nine disciplines in plain names; each discipline has its own page.

  3. 03EvaluateHow we work · Our Story

    The four stages every engagement follows, and the company’s history.

  4. 04Explore workCase Studies

    Systems in production. Each solution page links to the work where it was used.

  5. 05EnquireBook a discovery session

    A short form with one required decision: what are you looking to do?

Shortcut

Someone who is already convinced does not need the whole story. The action in the bar takes them straight to the enquiry from any page.

Alternative route

A visitor who arrives on a service page, from search for example, can go straight to the case studies where that discipline was used.

When something goes wrong

The form checks each field when the visitor leaves it. If a send fails, a summary at the top lists every problem, links each to its field and takes focus, so keyboard and screen-reader users land on it too.

These states are captured from the reference build. The build is a static site, so sending opens a pre-filled email and the page says so plainly. I have not verified here how the live implementation handles each state.

09·A key design decisionHow did you deal with constraints?

Clarity over complexity.

The business’s first preference was a futuristic site: extensive 3D, animation and scroll effects. The earliest build in the project repository explored exactly that, with a preloader, a real-time 3D object and scroll-driven sequences.

I evaluated that direction against who the site was for and what it had to do. People assessing a potential technology partner usually want to understand the company quickly. Heavy visual assets add performance risk, and connection quality varies, so an experience that depends on them is less dependable for some visitors. And motion that runs constantly competes with the information it surrounds.

I recommended a more restrained, performance-conscious direction, where the visual design supports the message rather than performing alongside it. The site still has character, in the gem, the hatch-line pictograms and the navy bands, but nothing stands between a visitor and the content.

  • Corporate visitors often need to understand capabilities quickly
  • Heavy visual assets introduce performance risk
  • Connectivity varies, so heavy experiences are less reliable for some visitors
  • Constant motion competes with the information people came for
  • The site had to feel professional, credible and direct

Original direction · 3D-heavy, highly animated

Design direction · clear, purposeful, performance-conscious

11·Developer collaboration and handoffHow did you work with other people?

Designing what can be built, then making it easy to build.

I worked with the full-stack developer from the early design stages, so technical constraints shaped the design before it reached handoff rather than after. Choices like a static site, and an enquiry that hands off to email, came from that conversation.

The development team was less familiar with navigating Figma. So, alongside the organised Figma files, I prepared a GitHub repository as an additional implementation reference: a working build of the design that anyone could open in a browser, or read as HTML and CSS.

The repository did not replace the team’s own codebase. It gave design and development a shared, inspectable reference: the tokens in one stylesheet, every page as it should look and behave, and written notes on the decisions behind them.

  1. FigmaScreens, design-system assets and reusable components, organised for handoff
  2. GitHub referenceThe design as a working static build: tokens, components, every page and its states
  3. DocumentationNotes on structure, decisions, accessibility and QA, kept with the build
  4. Development teamImplementation, with design reviews along the way

12·Implementation and quality assuranceHow did you know the work was ready?

Staying involved until the work was built.

After handoff I kept working with the development team to make sure the implementation matched the approved design, and I took part in testing. Before that, I tested the design itself in the reference build, so the problems I could find were fixed before they reached development.

A UX review, and what it changed

  • Four names for one action→One: Book a discovery session, everywhere
  • The form validated only on submit, with no summary→Checks on leaving a field, and an error summary that takes focus
  • The current page was not marked→Marked in the navigation, including the Solutions and Products triggers
  • Auto-advancing stages could not be paused→A pause control on each
  • Body copy at 14 pixels→16-pixel body, with line length held to a readable measure

These results describe the reference build, not the live site. I have not included a design-versus-live comparison, because the live site could not be captured for this page.

14·Reflection

What this project taught me.

The brief is a starting point, not the answer

The most useful thing I did was ask what the site was for before deciding what it should look like. That question turned a website request into brand work, and it gave me the grounds to recommend a calmer direction than the one first requested.

Technical companies need plain language

GEMIS does complex work. Its prospective clients mostly want to know whether it can solve their problem. Translating nine disciplines into outcomes a business owner recognises was as much design work as any screen.

Identity and experience are one piece of work

Building the brand and the website together meant the system was designed for real interface needs, such as states, fields and dense pages, not only for a logo sheet. That is also why it could be adopted.

Handoff is a design problem too

The team worked best from something they could open and read. Meeting them there with a working reference, and staying involved through testing, did more for implementation quality than another round of polished screens would have.

I approach design as a connected process — understanding the business, understanding the people it serves, structuring the experience, building the system behind the interface and staying involved until the work is implemented.
