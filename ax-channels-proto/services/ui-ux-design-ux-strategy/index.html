Strategy Decides. UX Structures. UI Communicates

Three Different Disciplines, In An Order That Matters. Each One Answers A Question The Next One Depends On.

  1. 01

    UX Strategy

    Who it is for, what they are trying to finish, what the business needs from it, and what should not be built at all.

    Are we building the right thing?

  2. 02

    UX Design

    Journeys, task flows, information architecture, navigation and content hierarchy, resolved as wireframes.

    Can someone actually finish?

  3. 03

    UI Design

    Layout, type, colour, components, visual hierarchy, interaction states and responsive behaviour.

    Is the next step obvious?

  4. 04

    Prototype

    The journeys that carry the most value, made clickable and walked through end to end.

    Does it hold up in use?

  5. 05

    Validation

    Usability testing and structured review where scope allows, then iteration on what comes back.

    What did we get wrong?

Not every engagement runs all five, and they are not always strictly sequential — validation usually sends you back a stage. What does not change is the dependency: a screen drawn before the structure is agreed is a guess, and an attractive guess is still a guess.

Most Of The Value Is In The Decisions Made Before The Design

UX Strategy Is The Part That Decides What The Experience Has To Do. It Is Also The Part Most Often Skipped.

The Questions It Answers

  • Who is this for? Named, specific, and narrow enough to design against.
  • What are they trying to finish? The task, not the feature list.
  • Where does it break down today? The points where people give up or ask for help.
  • What does the business need from it? Stated plainly, so design decisions can be held to it.
  • What comes first? A sequence, so the work is not held hostage to doing everything at once.
  • What should not be built? Usually the most valuable answer, and the hardest to get.

What It Produces

  • A UX audit of what exists, where there is something to audit.
  • Stakeholder and user insight, scoped to the engagement rather than implied.
  • Journey and experience maps showing the route people actually take.
  • Information architecture — what lives where, and what it is called.
  • Experience principles the team can use to settle arguments later.
  • A prioritised set of recommendations, with the reasoning attached.

Not every project needs every one of these, and we would rather scope two of them properly than list six and do them thinly. Where a strategy engagement is all you need, it can stop there — the output is useful on its own, whoever designs and builds from it.

Eight Stages, Scoped To The Problem

Each One Ends In Something You Can Look At And Argue With, So Direction Is Settled While It Is Still Cheap To Change.

  1. 01

    Understand

    The business, the people who use the thing, the context it runs in and what success would actually look like. Mostly listening, and asking what has already been tried.

    You get a written brief, scope and timeline.

  2. 02

    Research

    Stakeholder interviews, the analytics and support themes you already hold, competitive review, and users directly where the engagement includes it.

    You get findings, with their source stated.

  3. 03

    Define

    Which problems are worth solving, in what order, and the experience principles the rest of the work is held to.

    You get priorities and principles, agreed.

  4. 04

    Structure

    Information architecture, user and task flows, navigation and wireframes. The experience is decided here, in grey boxes, before anyone argues about a colour.

    You get an IA map, flows and wireframes.

  5. 05

    Design

    Interface design onto that structure: responsive layouts, interaction states, and the components the rest of the experience is assembled from.

    You get high-fidelity screens and a component set.

  6. 06

    Prototype

    The journeys that matter, made clickable and walked through end to end, so they can be judged as an experience rather than as screens.

    You get an interactive prototype.

  7. 07

    Validate

    Usability testing on the prototype and structured stakeholder review where scope allows, then iteration. Where it does not, we say so upfront rather than implying testing happened.

    You get findings and the changes made from them.

  8. 08

    Deliver

    Specified components, interaction behaviour, responsive rules and documentation, prepared for whoever implements it — and we stay reachable through the build.

    You get developer-ready files and documentation.

Consistency As Infrastructure

A System So The Next Screen Matches The Last One

Without one, every new screen is designed from memory, and an experience slowly stops feeling like one thing. A design system is the decisions written down once: components and their states, type, spacing, colour usage, interaction patterns and responsive rules. It keeps an experience coherent as it grows. It is not a promise about delivery speed or business results, and we will not sell it as one.

  • Components and their states

    Not just the default. Hover, focus, active, disabled, loading, error and empty, specified once.

  • Type and spacing

    A scale rather than a set of one-off values, so hierarchy is consistent wherever it appears.

  • Colour with rules attached

    What each colour means and where it is allowed, so emphasis keeps working as the palette gets used.

  • Forms, buttons and navigation

    The three things every product has most of, and the three that drift fastest without a system.

  • Interaction patterns

    How the same action behaves everywhere, so people can stop reading the interface and start using it.

  • Responsive rules

    How each component behaves as the space changes, decided in the system rather than per screen.

What is UX design?

UX design decides how an experience works: what happens, in what order, what things are called, and how someone gets from arriving to finishing. It produces journeys, flows, navigation, content hierarchy and wireframes — the structure an interface is later drawn onto.

What is UI design?

UI design decides how that experience looks, communicates and behaves: layout, type, colour, components, visual hierarchy, interaction states and responsive behaviour. Good UI makes a sound structure obvious; it cannot rescue an unsound one.

What is UX strategy?

UX strategy sits above both. UX design asks whether something is usable; UX strategy asks whether it is the right thing to build at all — who it is for, what they are trying to finish, what the business needs from it, and what should not be built.

What is the difference between UX and UI?

UX is the structure, UI is the surface. A product can have an attractive interface and still be hard to use, because the difficulty was decided before anyone opened a design tool. We work the order deliberately: strategy, then UX, then UI.

Do you conduct UX research?

Yes, scoped to the engagement. That can mean stakeholder interviews, reviewing analytics and support themes you already have, competitive review, or speaking to users directly. We say upfront which of those a project includes rather than implying a full research programme.

Can you audit an existing website or product?

Yes, and it is a good first engagement because it is bounded. You get prioritised findings with a severity on each, quick fixes separated from structural work, and a walk-through of the reasoning. Nothing is redesigned during an audit.

Do you design mobile apps?

Yes. Mobile journeys are designed to platform conventions rather than shrunk from a desktop layout, and the handoff covers interaction states and responsive behaviour.

Do you create prototypes?

Yes. The journeys that carry the most value are made clickable so they can be walked through, shown to stakeholders and tested before anyone builds them — the cheapest point at which to find out something does not work.

Do you create design systems?

Yes, either a new one or an extension of what you have. A system covers components and their states, typography, spacing, colour usage, interaction patterns and responsive rules. It keeps an experience consistent as it grows; it is not a promise about how fast anything gets built.

Can you work with our existing developers?

Yes, and it works better when they are in the conversation early, while structure is still being decided. Handover is specified components, interaction behaviour and responsive rules, and we stay reachable through the build.

Do you provide usability testing?

Where the engagement allows for it. Testing a prototype with a handful of people surfaces most of what is wrong; we will tell you honestly when a scope does not include it rather than implying that it does.

How much does UX/UI design cost?

There is no fixed price. A bounded UX audit and an end-to-end engagement across several journeys are not the same job. Scope is agreed in discovery and priced against it, in writing, before work starts. Websites are packaged and priced publicly; UX work is not.

How long does a UX/UI project take?

It depends on how many journeys are in scope, how settled the requirements are, and how quickly decisions come back. A timeline goes into your proposal once discovery has shown what the work involves. What we commit to upfront is a reply to any enquiry within two working days.
